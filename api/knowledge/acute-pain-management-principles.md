# Acute Pain Management Principles in Sickle Cell Disease

Acute painful episodes (vaso-occlusive crises) are the most common reason people with sickle cell disease (SCD) attend hospital. Public guidance from NHLBI, NICE and ASH agrees on a set of principles: fast assessment, fast and effective pain relief, individualised plans, frequent reassessment, and active prevention of complications. This document summarises those principles in general terms. It does not give drug doses; analgesic choice and dosing follow local protocols and the patient's individualised plan.

## Rapid triage and assessment

In sickle cell disease, a person presenting with acute pain should be triaged urgently and treated as a priority. Assessment should cover pain severity and character, how it compares with the person's usual crises, analgesia already taken at home, and a check for red flags: fever, chest symptoms or low oxygen, neurological signs, abdominal swelling or enlarged spleen, priapism and signs of severe anaemia. Pain that is atypical for the patient should prompt a search for other causes. Patients' reports of their pain should be believed and taken seriously.

## Speed of analgesia

In sickle cell disease, delays in pain relief are common and harmful, so guidelines set clear time targets:

- **NICE CG143** recommends offering analgesia within 30 minutes of arrival at hospital.
- **NHLBI (2014)** recommends rapid assessment and starting analgesic therapy (parenteral opioids for severe pain) within 30 minutes of triage or within 60 minutes of registration.

Services are expected to audit performance against these targets. Where a patient has an individualised pain plan, it should be used from the start.

## Individualised pain plans

In sickle cell disease, an individualised care plan written in advance with the patient and haematology team records what analgesia usually works for them, what has been tried at home, any allergies or adverse effects, and specific instructions for the emergency department. NHLBI and ASH both recommend individualised, patient-specific dosing protocols where possible because they lead to faster, more effective relief. Care teams should check whether such a plan exists at every presentation.

## Reassessment and titration

In sickle cell disease, pain, sedation and vital signs (including respiratory rate and oxygen saturation) should be reassessed frequently after analgesia is given. NHLBI advises reassessment and repeat dosing as needed every 15–30 minutes until pain is controlled; NICE advises monitoring at least every 30 minutes until pain is controlled and then regularly afterwards. Opioid use requires monitoring for sedation and slowed breathing, and patients should be offered treatment for side effects such as itching, nausea and constipation. Non-opioid analgesia may be used alongside opioids where appropriate and not contraindicated.

## Hydration

In sickle cell disease, people in crisis are often dehydrated. Fluid status should be assessed and fluids given by mouth or intravenously if the patient cannot drink enough. Guidance cautions against routine aggressive intravenous fluids, because over-hydration can contribute to fluid overload and acute chest syndrome. Fluid balance should be monitored.

## Preventing acute chest syndrome: incentive spirometry

In sickle cell disease, acute chest syndrome (ACS) often develops during a pain crisis, particularly with chest, back or abdominal pain, shallow breathing due to pain, or opioid-related sedation. NHLBI recommends incentive spirometry while awake for people hospitalised with a VOC to encourage deep breathing and reduce the risk of ACS. Staff should watch for new chest pain, cough, fever, rising breathing rate or falling oxygen saturation, and investigate promptly.

## Venous thromboembolism awareness

In sickle cell disease, people admitted to hospital have a raised risk of venous thromboembolism (blood clots in the veins or lungs) due to inflammation, reduced mobility and indwelling lines. Teams should assess VTE risk on admission according to local policy and consider prophylaxis, and should keep in mind that pulmonary embolism can mimic acute chest syndrome.

## Other supportive measures

In sickle cell disease, supportive measures during a painful episode include keeping the person warm, encouraging mobility when possible, offering oxygen only if saturation is low, and addressing anxiety. Blood transfusion is not used routinely for an uncomplicated VOC. On discharge, patients should have an analgesia plan, a follow-up arrangement and advice on when to return.
