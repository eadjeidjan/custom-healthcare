# Preventing Vaso-Occlusive Crises: Daily Self-Care

Many vaso-occlusive crises (VOCs) in sickle cell disease (SCD) cannot be prevented, but everyday habits can lower the chance of a crisis and help people notice problems early. This document sets out practical prevention steps that a care team can adapt into an individual patient plan. Advice should always be tailored to the person's age, other conditions and the plan agreed with their haematology team.

## Hydration in sickle cell disease

In sickle cell disease, staying well hydrated helps keep the blood less concentrated and reduces sickling. People with SCD are advised to drink fluids regularly through the day, not just when thirsty, and to drink more in hot weather, during exercise, during travel and when they are unwell with fever, vomiting or diarrhoea. The kidneys in SCD often cannot concentrate urine well, so fluid is lost more easily. Water is the best default choice. A specific daily target should be agreed with the care team, because fluid needs depend on body size, climate, activity and any heart or kidney problems. Pale urine is a simple self-check. Patients should seek advice if they cannot keep fluids down.

## Keeping warm and avoiding temperature extremes

In sickle cell disease, cold and sudden temperature changes are common crisis triggers. Practical steps include dressing in layers, wearing a hat, gloves and scarf in cold or windy weather, avoiding sitting in strong air conditioning, drying off quickly after bathing or rain, and avoiding swimming in cold water. If swimming, choose a heated pool, limit time in the water, and warm up and dry off straight away. Very hot conditions also matter because of fluid loss, so hydration should increase in heat.

## Pacing physical activity

In sickle cell disease, regular moderate activity is healthy and encouraged, but overexertion can trigger a crisis. People should warm up, take frequent rest breaks, drink before, during and after exercise, avoid exercising to exhaustion, and stop if they feel unwell, breathless or in pain. Schools, coaches and employers should know about the condition so rest and drinks are allowed.

## Sleep and stress management in sickle cell disease

In sickle cell disease, poor sleep and emotional stress are both associated with more pain. A regular sleep routine and enough sleep for age should be encouraged. Snoring, pauses in breathing or daytime sleepiness should be reported, as sleep-disordered breathing can lower oxygen levels and may need assessment. Stress management can include relaxation, breathing exercises, physical activity, peer support and psychological therapies such as cognitive behavioural approaches. Access to mental health support is part of good SCD care.

## Avoiding other triggers

In sickle cell disease, people are advised to avoid smoking (including second-hand smoke and vaping), limit or avoid alcohol because it dehydrates, and avoid recreational drugs. Women and girls may notice a pattern of pain around menstruation and should discuss this with their team. Pregnancy in SCD is high risk and needs planning with specialist obstetric and haematology care.

## Travel advice for people with sickle cell disease

In sickle cell disease, travel can combine several triggers. Before travel, people should discuss plans with their care team, carry a letter or summary of their condition and care plan, and ensure they have enough of their regular medicines. On flights they should drink regularly, avoid alcohol, keep warm, and move around the cabin. Unpressurised aircraft and high-altitude destinations carry a higher risk because of low oxygen and should be discussed in advance. Travellers to regions with malaria need appropriate prevention advice, as malaria is especially dangerous in SCD.

## Infection prevention: vaccines and penicillin

In sickle cell disease, the spleen stops working well early in life (functional asplenia), so serious bacterial infections are a major risk, especially in young children. Guidance (NHLBI, NICE) recommends:

- **Penicillin prophylaxis** for children with HbSS (and in many protocols other severe genotypes) from early infancy until at least five years of age, as prescribed by the care team.
- **Vaccinations** – the full routine childhood schedule plus pneumococcal vaccines, and meningococcal, influenza and, where recommended, COVID-19 vaccines, kept up to date into adulthood.
- Good hand hygiene, and prompt medical review for any fever.

## Regular review and early warning

In sickle cell disease, regular reviews with the haematology team allow monitoring of blood counts, organ function and treatment adherence. Children with HbSS or HbSβ0 should have annual transcranial Doppler (TCD) screening (generally from age 2 to 16) to detect stroke risk. Patients should take disease-modifying medicines such as hydroxyurea as prescribed, know their own baseline haemoglobin, and have a written individualised care plan that says what to do for mild pain at home and when to seek urgent help. Fever, chest symptoms, stroke-like symptoms or uncontrolled pain always need urgent medical attention.
