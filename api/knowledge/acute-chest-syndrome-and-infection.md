# Acute Chest Syndrome and Infection in Sickle Cell Disease

Acute chest syndrome (ACS) and serious bacterial infection are two of the most dangerous complications of sickle cell disease (SCD). Both can begin with subtle signs and worsen quickly, and both are closely linked with vaso-occlusive crises. This document explains what they are, what triggers them, the warning signs to watch for, and why early recognition and prevention matter.

## What acute chest syndrome is

In sickle cell disease, acute chest syndrome is defined as a new area of lung shadowing (infiltrate or consolidation) on a chest X-ray, together with one or more of: fever, cough, chest pain, fast breathing, wheeze or low oxygen levels. It is caused by a combination of infection, blockage of small lung vessels by sickled cells, and fat embolism from bone marrow affected by a crisis. ACS is a leading cause of hospital admission to intensive care and of death in SCD, and repeated episodes can cause chronic lung damage.

## Triggers and risk factors for acute chest syndrome

In sickle cell disease, ACS frequently develops during a hospital admission for a pain crisis, often one to three days after it starts. Recognised risk factors include:

- a vaso-occlusive crisis, especially with chest, back, rib or abdominal pain;
- shallow breathing because of pain, or sedation from opioids;
- respiratory infections, including viral infections and atypical bacteria;
- recent surgery or general anaesthesia;
- asthma or a previous history of ACS;
- HbSS genotype, low fetal haemoglobin and high baseline white cell count;
- smoking and second-hand smoke.

## Warning signs of acute chest syndrome

In sickle cell disease, the following should raise concern for ACS: new chest pain, cough, shortness of breath, breathing faster than usual, fever, wheeze, or a fall in oxygen saturation compared with the person's baseline. Signs can be mild at first. Any of these, especially during a pain crisis, should prompt urgent medical assessment and a chest X-ray. People at home with these symptoms should seek emergency care rather than wait.

## Why incentive spirometry and early recognition matter

In sickle cell disease, deep breathing helps keep the lungs fully inflated and reduces the risk of ACS. NHLBI recommends incentive spirometry (a simple device that encourages slow deep breaths) for people admitted with a pain crisis, and it is also used around surgery. Early recognition matters because ACS can progress rapidly to respiratory failure; prompt treatment with antibiotics, oxygen when needed, careful fluid management, pain control and blood transfusion when indicated improves outcomes. Hydroxyurea reduces the frequency of ACS and is recommended after severe or recurrent episodes.

## Why infection risk is high in sickle cell disease

In sickle cell disease, repeated sickling damages the spleen from early childhood, so most people with HbSS lose normal spleen function (functional asplenia), often in the first years of life. The spleen normally clears encapsulated bacteria from the blood, so people with SCD are at high risk of overwhelming infection and sepsis from organisms such as *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae* type b and *Neisseria meningitidis*. *Salmonella* is a recognised cause of bone infection (osteomyelitis) in SCD. Malaria is particularly dangerous in SCD in endemic regions. Before penicillin prophylaxis and vaccination, infection was a major cause of death in young children with SCD.

## Fever is an emergency in sickle cell disease

In sickle cell disease, fever must be treated as a possible serious infection until proven otherwise. NHLBI recommends urgent evaluation for a temperature of 38.5 °C (101.3 °F) or higher, including history and examination, full blood count with reticulocytes, blood culture and, in many protocols, prompt parenteral antibiotics; many local and paediatric protocols use 38.0 °C (100.4 °F). Fever may also be the first sign of ACS. Patients should not delay seeking care or treat a fever at home without advice.

## Preventing infection in sickle cell disease

In sickle cell disease, infection prevention includes daily penicillin prophylaxis for young children (at least until age five), keeping routine and additional vaccinations (pneumococcal, meningococcal, Hib, influenza and others as recommended) up to date, malaria prevention for travellers and residents of endemic areas, and education so families recognise fever early.
