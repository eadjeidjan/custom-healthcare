# Laboratory Tests and Monitoring in Sickle Cell Disease

Blood tests and simple observations help care teams understand how sickle cell disease (SCD) is behaving in an individual, spot complications early, and monitor treatment. Because "normal" values in SCD differ from those in the general population and from person to person, results are best interpreted against the patient's own steady-state baseline. This document explains the common tests and what changes may suggest a crisis or complication is developing.

## Haemoglobin compared with baseline

In sickle cell disease, people usually live with chronic anaemia, and steady-state haemoglobin varies by genotype (typically lower in HbSS and HbSβ0 than in HbSC). The key question is how today's haemoglobin compares with the person's own baseline. A fall of about 2 g/dL or more below baseline is commonly treated as significant and warrants looking for a cause such as splenic sequestration, aplastic crisis, increased haemolysis, acute chest syndrome or bleeding. Patients and teams should record the steady-state value in the care plan.

## Reticulocyte count

In sickle cell disease, the reticulocyte count (young red cells) is usually high because the marrow works hard to replace short-lived sickle cells. A rise above baseline suggests increased haemolysis or recovery after a crisis. A low reticulocyte count with a falling haemoglobin is a warning sign of aplastic crisis, often caused by parvovirus B19, and needs urgent assessment. Reticulocytes should always be read together with haemoglobin.

## Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and bilirubin

In sickle cell disease, LDH and unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin are markers of red cell breakdown (haemolysis). Both are usually raised at baseline. A rise above the person's usual level can accompany a vaso-occlusive crisis or increased haemolysis; higher LDH has been associated with tissue damage during crises and with some chronic complications. Jaundice or a sharp rise in bilirubin with abdominal pain may also point to gallstones or liver complications.

## White blood cell count

In sickle cell disease, the baseline white blood cell (WBC) count is often higher than in people without SCD, and a high steady-state WBC is linked with more severe disease, more crises and acute chest syndrome. A WBC well above baseline, particularly with fever, raises concern for infection. Hydroxyurea typically lowers the WBC, and very low counts during treatment need review by the haematology team.

## Fetal haemoglobin (HbF)

In sickle cell disease, fetal haemoglobin protects against sickling. Higher HbF levels are associated with fewer crises and better outcomes. HbF is measured at diagnosis and periodically, and a rise is one sign that hydroxyurea is working and being taken regularly. A falling HbF in someone on hydroxyurea may suggest missed doses.

## Oxygen saturation (SpO2)

In sickle cell disease, pulse oximetry gives a quick, non-invasive measure of oxygen saturation. Some people with SCD have a slightly lower baseline SpO2. A drop compared with baseline, or a value below about 94% in many protocols, especially with chest symptoms or fever, should prompt urgent assessment for acute chest syndrome. Oximeters can be less accurate in severe anaemia and on darker skin, so readings should be interpreted with clinical signs.

## Patterns that suggest a crisis is developing

In sickle cell disease, no single lab value predicts a crisis, but some combinations are concerning:

- rising LDH, bilirubin and reticulocytes with a falling haemoglobin — increased haemolysis;
- falling haemoglobin with low reticulocytes — possible aplastic crisis;
- sudden fall in haemoglobin with an enlarged spleen — possible splenic sequestration;
- raised WBC with fever — possible infection;
- falling SpO2 with chest symptoms — possible acute chest syndrome.

Lab trends should support, not replace, clinical assessment of symptoms.

## Routine monitoring in sickle cell disease

In sickle cell disease, people are reviewed regularly by their specialist team, generally at least once or twice a year when well and more often when starting or adjusting treatment. Routine checks commonly include full blood count and reticulocytes, kidney and liver function, urine testing for protein, and HbF. Hydroxyurea requires regular blood counts at intervals set by the haematology team. Screening also includes annual transcranial Doppler for children with HbSS or HbSβ0 (usually ages 2–16), regular eye checks for retinopathy from later childhood, blood pressure, and iron monitoring for people receiving regular transfusions.
