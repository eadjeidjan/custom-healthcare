# Red Flags in Sickle Cell Disease: When to Seek Emergency Care

Some symptoms in sickle cell disease (SCD) can signal life-threatening complications that need urgent assessment, often within hours. This document lists the main red flags, explains why each matters, and gives a clear action for patients and care teams. In SickleGuard, any of these red flags overrides the risk score and is shown as critical with the advice to seek emergency care.

## Fever in sickle cell disease

In sickle cell disease, fever is a medical emergency because reduced spleen function makes overwhelming bacterial infection (sepsis) possible, especially in children. NHLBI guidance uses a temperature of 38.5 °C (101.3 °F) or higher as the threshold for urgent evaluation; many local and paediatric protocols use a lower threshold of 38.0 °C. Care teams should follow their local protocol.

**Action:** For fever at or above the threshold in the patient's care plan, seek urgent medical assessment the same day (emergency department or day unit). Do not wait to see if it settles. Blood tests, blood cultures and prompt antibiotics are usually needed.

## Chest pain, breathlessness or low oxygen (acute chest syndrome)

In sickle cell disease, chest pain, cough, fast or difficult breathing, or low oxygen saturation (SpO2 below the person's usual level, or below about 94% in many protocols) can indicate acute chest syndrome (ACS), a leading cause of death in SCD. ACS can develop during a pain crisis or after surgery.

**Action:** Seek emergency care immediately. Do not manage at home.

## Stroke signs in sickle cell disease

In sickle cell disease, stroke can occur in children and adults. Warning signs include sudden weakness or numbness of the face, arm or leg (especially on one side), facial droop, slurred speech or trouble understanding, sudden severe headache, seizure, confusion, loss of balance, or sudden vision loss.

**Action:** Call emergency services immediately (999/112/911 as locally applicable). Stroke in SCD is treated urgently, often with exchange transfusion, and time matters.

## Priapism in sickle cell disease

In sickle cell disease, priapism is a painful, unwanted erection. Short episodes can be managed at home with measures agreed with the care team (such as passing urine, drinking fluids and gentle exercise). An episode lasting more than about 2 hours, per many patient guidance sources, or 4 hours, per NHLBI's threshold for urgent urology assessment, risks permanent damage.

**Action:** If an episode lasts longer than the time in the care plan (commonly 2 hours, never more than 4), go to the emergency department. Recurrent short episodes should be reported to the team.

## Sudden pallor, fatigue or an enlarged spleen

In sickle cell disease, sudden paleness, unusual tiredness, weakness, fast heartbeat, or a swollen, tender left side of the abdomen can indicate splenic sequestration (blood pooling in the spleen) or an aplastic crisis (the bone marrow stops making red cells, often after parvovirus B19 infection). Both cause a rapid, dangerous fall in haemoglobin. Parents of young children are often taught to feel for the spleen.

**Action:** Seek emergency care the same day. A blood count and reticulocyte count are needed, and transfusion may be required.

## Severe pain not controlled by the home plan

In sickle cell disease, pain that is severe, unusual in character or location, or not relieved by the individualised home pain plan needs prompt hospital assessment and fast analgesia.

**Action:** Go to hospital or the day unit as directed by the care plan. Bring the care plan or details of usual medicines.

## Abdominal swelling or severe abdominal pain

In sickle cell disease, a swollen or hard abdomen, severe abdominal pain, or vomiting may signal splenic sequestration, gallstone complications, bowel problems or other serious causes.

**Action:** Seek urgent medical assessment.

## Vision changes

In sickle cell disease, sudden loss of vision, new floaters or flashes may indicate retinal problems or stroke and need urgent assessment.

**Action:** Seek emergency care for sudden vision loss; contact eye services the same day for new floaters or flashes.

## Other urgent situations

In sickle cell disease, dehydration with inability to keep fluids down, severe headache, unusual drowsiness, reduced urine output, and any concern in pregnancy should also prompt urgent contact with the care team.
