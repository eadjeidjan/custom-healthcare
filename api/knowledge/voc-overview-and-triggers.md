# Vaso-Occlusive Crisis (VOC): Overview, Triggers and Risk Markers

A vaso-occlusive crisis (VOC), often called a sickle cell "pain crisis", is the most common reason people with sickle cell disease (SCD) seek urgent care. This document explains what happens during a VOC, how common it is, what tends to trigger it, and which clinical and laboratory markers are linked with a higher risk of crises. It is intended to help care teams explain risk to patients and to support prevention planning.

## What a vaso-occlusive crisis is

In sickle cell disease, red blood cells carry an abnormal haemoglobin (haemoglobin S). When haemoglobin S gives up its oxygen, it can polymerise, making red cells stiff, sticky and sickle-shaped. These cells, together with activated white cells, platelets and the inflamed lining of blood vessels, can block small blood vessels. The blockage reduces blood flow and oxygen delivery to tissues, causing ischaemia, inflammation and severe pain. A VOC is this episode of acute pain caused by blocked microcirculation, most often felt in the back, chest, arms, legs and joints, though it can affect almost any part of the body. A VOC is not "just pain": repeated episodes of vaso-occlusion contribute to organ damage over time, and a VOC can evolve into serious complications such as acute chest syndrome.

## How common vaso-occlusive crises are

In sickle cell disease, VOCs are the hallmark acute complication and the leading cause of emergency department visits and hospital admissions. Frequency varies widely between people: some have very few crises a year, while others have several or many. People with higher crisis frequency tend to have more complications and, in older studies, higher mortality, which is why crisis frequency is used both as a marker of disease severity and as a reason to start disease-modifying therapy. Many people with SCD also have pain at home that never reaches hospital, so hospital records underestimate the true burden.

## Common triggers of a vaso-occlusive crisis

In sickle cell disease, a VOC often has no obvious cause, but several triggers are well recognised by patients and clinical guidance (NHLBI, NICE, CDC):

- **Dehydration** – concentrates the blood and increases sickling; hot weather, vomiting, diarrhoea and not drinking enough all contribute.
- **Cold, wind and sudden temperature change** – cold causes blood vessels to constrict; cold wind and moving between hot and cold environments are frequently reported triggers.
- **Swimming in cold water** – combines cold exposure, exertion and sometimes breath-holding.
- **Infection and fever** – infection drives inflammation and is a major trigger, and fever itself is a medical emergency in SCD.
- **Low oxygen (hypoxia)** – from lung illness, sleep-disordered breathing or high altitude.
- **High altitude and unpressurised flights** – lower oxygen levels can provoke sickling; commercial pressurised flights are usually fine with precautions.
- **Physical overexertion** – intense or prolonged exercise without rest and fluids.
- **Emotional stress** – psychological stress is commonly linked with crises.
- **Alcohol** – contributes to dehydration.
- **Smoking** – harms the lungs and is associated with acute chest syndrome and more pain.
- **Menstruation and pregnancy** – hormonal changes are associated with pain; pregnancy carries increased crisis and complication risk.
- **Poor sleep** – lack of sleep and sleep-disordered breathing are associated with more pain.

## Markers associated with higher VOC risk

In sickle cell disease, several patient characteristics are associated with more frequent or severe crises:

- **Genotype** – HbSS and HbSβ0-thalassaemia generally have the most severe course; HbSC and HbSβ+-thalassaemia are usually milder, though serious complications still occur.
- **Low fetal haemoglobin (HbF)** – HbF inhibits sickling; lower HbF levels are linked with more crises.
- **High baseline white blood cell count** – associated with more severe disease and complications.
- **Prior crisis frequency** – frequent crises in the past year are among the strongest predictors of future crises.
- **Hydroxyurea non-adherence** – missed doses reduce the protective rise in HbF.
- **Higher baseline haemoglobin** – in some studies associated with more pain episodes (thought to relate to blood viscosity), so haemoglobin must be interpreted against the person's own baseline.

## Laboratory changes that may signal a developing VOC

In sickle cell disease, trends matter more than single values. A rise in lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) and reticulocyte count, rising bilirubin, and a fall in haemoglobin below the patient's usual baseline suggest increased haemolysis or marrow stress and can accompany or precede a crisis. A falling haemoglobin with low reticulocytes may instead suggest an aplastic crisis, and a sudden fall with an enlarged spleen may indicate splenic sequestration; both are emergencies. Lab trends should always be read alongside symptoms.
