# Disease-Modifying Therapy in Sickle Cell Disease

Disease-modifying therapies aim to reduce how often vaso-occlusive crises (VOCs) and other complications happen in sickle cell disease (SCD), rather than only treating pain when it occurs. This document summarises the main options and their regulatory status. It does not give doses: the choice of therapy, dosing and monitoring are set and supervised by the patient's haematology team.

## Hydroxyurea in sickle cell disease

In sickle cell disease, hydroxyurea (also called hydroxycarbamide) is the most established disease-modifying medicine. It increases fetal haemoglobin (HbF), which slows sickling, and also lowers white cell counts and improves blood flow. The landmark adult trial (MSH) and the paediatric trial (BABY HUG) showed that hydroxyurea reduces painful crises, acute chest syndrome, hospital admissions and the need for blood transfusion, and long-term follow-up suggests improved survival.

NHLBI 2014 guidance recommends offering hydroxyurea to:

- infants from 9 months of age, children and adolescents with HbSS or HbSβ0-thalassaemia, regardless of how severe their symptoms are;
- adults with HbSS or HbSβ0 who have three or more moderate-to-severe pain crises in a 12-month period, pain that interferes with daily life, a history of severe or recurrent acute chest syndrome, or severe symptomatic chronic anaemia.

Use in other genotypes (such as HbSC) is decided case by case by specialists.

## Hydroxyurea adherence and monitoring

In sickle cell disease, hydroxyurea only works if taken regularly, and benefits build up over months. Missed doses reduce the HbF response, so adherence is an important part of crisis prevention and a useful topic in every review. Hydroxyurea can lower blood counts, so the haematology team checks full blood counts and reticulocytes regularly — more often when starting or changing treatment and less often once stable. Patients should not stop or change their dose without speaking to their team, and should discuss pregnancy plans because hydroxyurea is usually avoided in pregnancy.

## L-glutamine in sickle cell disease

In sickle cell disease, L-glutamine oral powder (Endari) was approved by the US FDA in 2017 for patients aged five and over to reduce acute complications. It is thought to reduce oxidative stress in red cells. Its trial showed fewer pain crises, but the evidence is more limited than for hydroxyurea, and the European Medicines Agency did not grant marketing authorisation. Availability varies by country.

## Crizanlizumab in sickle cell disease

In sickle cell disease, crizanlizumab (Adakveo) is an antibody given by infusion that blocks P-selectin, a molecule involved in cells sticking to blood vessel walls. It received FDA approval in 2019 based on the SUSTAIN trial, which showed fewer crises. However, the larger confirmatory STAND trial did not show a significant reduction in crises compared with placebo. As a result, the EU marketing authorisation was revoked in 2023. Its status in other regions should be checked locally.

## Voxelotor in sickle cell disease

In sickle cell disease, voxelotor (Oxbryta) increases haemoglobin's affinity for oxygen to reduce polymerisation, and it was approved in 2019 on the basis of a rise in haemoglobin. In September 2024 Pfizer voluntarily withdrew voxelotor from all markets worldwide, after newer data suggested the overall benefit no longer outweighed the risks, including an imbalance in vaso-occlusive crises and deaths in later studies. Patients previously on voxelotor should be reviewed by their haematology team. It should not be presented as a current treatment option.

## Blood transfusion in sickle cell disease

In sickle cell disease, regular (chronic) red cell transfusion lowers the proportion of sickle haemoglobin. It is the established approach for primary stroke prevention in children with abnormal transcranial Doppler results and for secondary prevention after stroke, and may be used for other severe complications. Transfusion carries risks including iron overload, alloimmunisation and reactions, so it requires specialist management, extended red cell matching and monitoring of iron levels.

## Gene therapies and curative options

In sickle cell disease, haematopoietic stem cell transplantation from a matched donor can be curative, particularly in children with a matched sibling donor. In December 2023 the US FDA approved two gene therapies for people aged 12 and over with recurrent VOCs: exagamglogene autotemcel (exa-cel, Casgevy), a CRISPR-based therapy that boosts HbF, and lovotibeglogene autotemcel (lovo-cel, Lyfgenia), which adds a modified gene to produce an anti-sickling haemoglobin. Casgevy was also authorised in the UK (November 2023) and the EU. These treatments require stem cell collection and high-intensity chemotherapy conditioning, are delivered in specialist centres, and are very costly, so access is limited.
